01 / The problemAI cinema has no shelf

More finished AI cinema exists this year than in the last three combined. There is nowhere to put it

01

Feeds have no memory

A finished film is published as a scroll. It peaks in about thirty-six hours and then it does not exist. Nothing accumulates, so nothing compounds.

02

Festivals don't pay rent

The AI festival circuit is real and growing, and it is a prize economy. Selected, screened, laurel, over. No recurring revenue for the filmmaker.

03

Nobody can find the good work

Volume has outrun discovery. There is no trusted mark that says this one is worth forty minutes of your life.

02 / Why nowThree forcing functions

The window is opening right now, and it will not stay open

  • 01Model quality crossed the threshold. Seedance 2.5, Veo 3.1, Kling 3.0, Runway Gen-4.5, MiniMax H3 and Wan 3.0 can now carry a 40-minute narrative. Two years ago this catalogue could not exist.
  • 02Provenance stopped being optional. The EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations applied from 2 August 2026 — AI-generated and manipulated content distributed in the EU must be disclosed, with machine-readable marking required of generative systems and a transition to 2 December 2026. A curated platform that labels properly is now a compliance asset, not a nicety.
  • 03Supply is being manufactured at scale. The festival circuit — Runway's AIF, Project Odyssey, Curious Refuge, the independent AI festivals — is now producing a large, annual, self-identifying cohort of filmmakers with finished work and no home for it.
03 / The wedgeSelection, not generation

Generation is being commoditised. Selection is the scarce good

Every month the models get cheaper, faster and more available. The studios and the model labs are all building the same showcase onto their own tools.

What none of them is building is a shelf — a single place with a taste, a spine number, a permanent slot and a payout.

Criterion's value was never the projector. It was the sentence explaining why the film was chosen, and the promise that the choice was made by a person. That business is fifty years old and still charges more than Netflix.

Netflix solved distribution and lost the shelf. Feeds solved reach and never had one. Festivals have taste and no persistence. Venice Oscura is the shelf.

04 / Why Venice builds thisThe retention argument

Model access is commoditising. A culture around the output is not

01 · Retention

Nobody cancels the thing they also watch

An aggregator competes on model parity, and parity arrives for everybody within weeks. Oscura gives Pro a reason to survive the month a competitor matches the model list — the subscription becomes a habit rather than a utility bill.

02 · Funnel

Every film is an advert for the stack that made it

Each film lists every model that made it. Most of those models are rentable inside Venice, so the disclosure is also the call to action: watch it, read how it was made, open the same stack.

03 · Defensibility

Any lab can add Seedance tomorrow

None of them can add a catalogue with a taste, a filmmaker roster on non-exclusive terms, and a production fund the audience controls. That is the part that takes three years and cannot be bought.

The flywheel

Watch → see what it was made with → open the stack → make → submit → pitch the next one → get funded

Every arrow in that loop ends somewhere Venice already monetises. The collection is the showroom; Pro is the workshop; the slate is what brings a filmmaker back rather than losing them after one film.

What it costs Venice

An internal allocation, not new spend

The model assumes $2.50 of each $18 Pro subscription is booked against Oscura — roughly 14%, the share of why somebody stays. Set it to zero and the standalone business still works; it is simply slower.

Venice pricing, model line-up and the 230+ figure are taken from Venice's published pricing page and changelogs and will drift. The allocation is a proposal, not an agreed internal transfer price. No part of this has been agreed with Venice.

06 / The slateViewers choose what gets made

The only streaming service where the audience hands a filmmaker a budget

  • 01Filmmakers pitch with key art. A one-sheet, a line and a logline. Twelve films sit on the slate at a time, and anyone can browse them.
  • 02Subscribers get five signals a month. They reset on the 1st and do not roll over. A vote you cannot run out of is a like, and likes measure reach rather than regard.
  • 03The top three each quarter are funded. 12% of net pools quarterly. At 25,000 direct subscribers and 40,000 Pro viewers that is $91,459 a quarter across three films.
  • 04It never touches a payout. What a released film earns is settled privately by formula. The vote only decides what gets made next, so the collection never becomes a leaderboard.
$91,459
Slate fund per quarter at the modelled base case, paid from the platform's share and not from the filmmakers' pool
Why it matters commercially

It makes subscribing consequential

A subscriber with five signals and a quarterly deadline has a reason to come back and care which films are near the line. That is retention, and it costs 12% of net.

Why it matters for supply

It answers "then what?"

Every other home for AI film pays for work already made. This one funds the next film, which is what a filmmaker with a finished short actually needs.

Slate entries and signal counts in the comp are placeholder data. The quarterly fund is an output of the same model as every other figure in this deck; nothing has been funded.
07 / SupplyThe deal that recruits filmmakers

A filmmaker only needs one reason to say yes, and one reason to say no. We remove the reason to say no

Half of net subscription revenue goes into the filmmakers' pool. A quarter of that pool is split equally across every title in the collection — a floor for being selected, paid whether or not anyone watches this month.

The remaining three quarters follow each subscriber's own viewing, weighted by completion. Subscriber money is not pooled across the whole catalogue, so a small devoted audience pays a filmmaker better than a large indifferent one.

The licence is non-exclusive. Copyright stays with the filmmaker. They keep posting everywhere, on the same day. Terminable on 30 days' notice, no penalty.

08 / Business modelThree lines, in sequence

Pro retention pays for the collection. Licensing is where the margin is

Line one · the reason Venice funds it

Venice Pro allocation

Oscura is bundled into Pro at $18. The model books $2.50 of each Pro subscription against the collection — an internal transfer, carrying no card fees, justified by retention rather than by new cash.

Line two · live at launch

Direct subscription

$6.99 a month or $69 a year for people who want the collection and not the studio. Half of net goes to the filmmakers' pool. This is what proves the thing stands on its own.

Line three · the actual thesis

Curated package licensing

The non-exclusive licence already grants the right to package the collection onward — FAST channels, airlines, telcos, hotels, film schools. This is a media rights business, and the reason to gather 1,000 non-exclusive titles rather than 30 exclusive ones.

Licensing revenue is unmodelled and unpriced: the rights grant is drafted, but no licence has been negotiated and no rate card exists — treat line three as the thesis under test, not a forecast. The Pro allocation is a proposed internal transfer price and has not been agreed with Venice.

10 / Go to marketSupply first, and it is not close

Recruit 100 filmmakers before we try to recruit one subscriber. The first forty are already Venice users

  • 01Start inside the house. Venice already bills thousands of people who generate video every week. A single in-product message to Pro subscribers who use the video models reaches a self-identifying filmmaker cohort that costs nothing to find — and the free shelf gives them something to show people before they have been accepted.
  • 02Then mine the festival cohorts. Runway AIF, Project Odyssey, Curious Refuge and the independent AI festivals publish their selections. These filmmakers have finished work, a public identity and no distribution. Approach them directly, with the payout formula and the licence in hand.
  • 03Publish the deal, not the brand. A page that states the split, the rights and the route to funding in plain words outperforms any amount of positioning copy with this audience. The comp's filmmakers page is three promises and a form.
  • 04Trade curation for promotion. Offer the strongest filmmakers a jury seat or a curated collection. It costs nothing, it recruits their audience, and it builds the taste brand in public.

11 / What must be trueRisks, stated plainly

Four things have to hold. Three of them we control

The riskVerdictWhat we do about it
Filmmakers won't licence non-exclusively We control They already do this for festivals, for YouTube and for Vimeo. The floor plus a real pool beats a laurel. The filmmakers page in the comp makes this argument in three lines.
Consumers won't pay for AI film at all Testable cheaply This is the central unknown and the reason Phase 0 ships a real catalogue before any build spend on accounts. If nobody pays, we have lost weeks rather than a round — and the licensing line survives without a consumer business.
Venice owning the shelf taints the curation Partly The sharpest objection to the plan, and inside Venice it is ours: a company selling model access has every incentive to favour films made with its own models, and an audience that suspects it stops trusting the shelf — which is the whole asset. The commitments are structural: tool-blind selection against a published rule, a label that discloses the stack without scoring it, and identical terms, payouts and slate eligibility for non-Venice work. Venice is a multi-vendor aggregator rather than a single lab, which narrows the conflict without removing it.
Churn kills the subscription maths Genuinely uncertain Above roughly 8% monthly churn the model does not work at $6.99. We measure it before we scale spend, and we treat the annual plan — not new titles — as the primary retention lever.

A4 / MoatWhat compounds

None of this is a technology moat. All of it compounds

01

A taste that becomes a mark

The selection is the brand. If an Oscura spine number reliably means "worth your time", the mark does the discovery work for every title we add. This is the only asset that gets stronger with age.

02

A filmmaker roster with a budget attached

Nobody else offers a guaranteed floor, zero exclusivity, and a production fund the audience votes on. The roster is cheap to hold, impossible to lift, and improves as the payout grows.

03

A provenance and compliance layer

A consistent, machine-readable model-stack disclosure across a whole catalogue is a genuine operational asset under Article 50 — and a service tools and studios will pay for.

04

Licensable rights, aggregated

A thousand non-exclusive titles clear for onward licensing is a catalogue no competitor can assemble quickly. That is the asset the second revenue line is built on.

The real risk is not competition — it is indifference. Model labs will keep building showcases, and they will keep not being a shelf. The difference here is that Venice already has the distribution, the filmmakers and the models; what it does not yet have is a reason for any of them to stay once a competitor matches the model list. This is that reason.

At 25,000 subscribers watching 9 hours a month — 225,000 viewing hours — the delivery stack is the difference between $18k and $162k a year.

Managed video APIs buy back enormous engineering time and charge up to 9× the egress for it. At launch, that trade is correct. Past roughly 10,000 subscribers it is not.

Plan: start on a managed API to ship in weeks, then move to self-encoded HLS with AV1/H.265 renditions on a zero-egress object store behind a cheap CDN — architected for the migration from day one, because the source-of-truth is the master file, not the provider.

Egress and delivery rates read from published provider pricing and a 2026 video-cost survey in September 2026; they change often, so re-price before committing. AV1/H.265 typically cuts delivered bytes 40–70% and is the single largest cost lever after the CDN choice.
